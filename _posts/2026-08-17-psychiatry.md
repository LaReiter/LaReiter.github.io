Psychiatry is hard.

This became clear to me when I started at Copenhagen Research Centre for Precision Psychiatry and thereby swapping analysis of animals for analysis of people.

Like animals, two persons can differ considerably from one another.

But what I also realized these first few months is just how hard the core mission of psychiatry is, which is to diagnose and treat patients with psychiatric illness. 

A patient’s symptom presentation is rarely stable and can change within days.

A clinician who assesses the patient will likely also possess some internal ‘noise’ that can shift the clinical judgment. In attention, in mood and what they happened to notice the week before.

Another clinician might take a different viewpoint on that very same patient.

Finally, the very act of bringing a patient to a clinic might not be representative of the ‘real world’ where the patient spends by far most of their daily life.

Putting this all together and the complexity becomes apparent.

Because patients differ from each other, care has to (ideally) be built around the individual. But even when we zoom in on the individual, the assessment rests on shifting ground: which days we observed the patient and how the patient’s presentation changes in the clinic. Which clinician was used and what state the clinician was in.

Getting to the right diagnosis, and matching that diagnosis to the right treatment, is to put it mildly, a difficult task.

It is also one I look forward to studying, with care and precision.
